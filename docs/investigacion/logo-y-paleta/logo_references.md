# Reference marks for Servos: an open Bible/book that is both written and spoken

Scope: professional vector marks that are useful references for Servos (offline-first PWA for Bible translation teams
in Latin America, written + oral/audio translation). Research date: 2026-10-17.

How to read these notes:

- References are numbered R1–R34 so the report writer can cite them. Each has: idea, reduction, colour, scaling,
  why it works (or doesn't), and a URL where the mark can be seen.
- "(observed)" means I looked at the mark myself in a browser on 2026-10-17 at the URL given; the description is
  my own visual reading, not a claim made by the brand. Everything else is attributed to the linked source.
- Many sector marks were observed on one page that shows about 40 Bible-agency logos side by side:
  [Forum of Bible Agencies International – Members](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/) (abbreviated
  "FOBAI members page" below). It's the most useful single page for seeing what the sector looks like.
- Years are given where a source states them. Older/classic marks are flagged as such.

## 1. Which professionally designed marks depict an open book / reading / library / publishing in a reduced, ownable way?

### Takeaway
The good book marks don't draw "a book". They make the book a container or a gesture: DK puts its monogram inside a
simplified one-colour open book, Darien Library shows pages being flipped, and Roxane Gay Books turns a letter into a
page/door. They're ownable because something specific (a monogram, a motion, a letter) does the work. The open
book itself is generic.

### Cited Findings

**R1. DK (Dorling Kindersley), by Pentagram (partner Angus Hyland). Current.**
- Idea: the logo keeps DK's central open-book motif, which houses the "DK" monogram — [Pentagram case study](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story)
- Reduction: an evolution of the previous logo, refined and simplified, with the monogram redrawn in bespoke modern serif letterforms — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story)
- Colour: now a single colour, so it can be printed in whatever colour and finish suits each book cover — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story)
- Scaling: Pentagram says the logo works at all scales, with a flexible but consistent system for its placement on covers — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story)
- Pentagram also designed the previous version, so this is a refinement, not a replacement (the page gives no year) — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story)
- Why it works: the book is a frame and the letters are the content. It's a direct analogue for Servos, where the book "contains" a translation.

**R2. Darien Library (Connecticut), by C&G Partners, led by Steff Geissbuhler. Classic (c. 2007).**
- Idea: the mark comes from the simple act of flipping a book's pages, drawn as a progression of transparent colour tints; it also reads as a wave, leaves or a bird's wing, suggesting movement and ascent — [Geissbuhler project page](https://geissbuhler.com/darien-library)
- The brief asked for an identity that was timeless yet flexible enough for changes in technology, service and media — [Geissbuhler](https://geissbuhler.com/darien-library)
- Reception: a 2007 blog post quoting Brand New's coverage called the icon delightful but criticised the typography and the dropped space between the two words — [Tame the Web, 2007](https://tametheweb.com/2007/11/22/the-evolution-of-a-library-logo/)
- Why it works: it shows a motion (page-turning) rather than an object, and the same curve also reads as a wave, which is close to the "sound" idea. Weakness: it relies on transparent tints, so a one-colour version loses the layering.

**R3. Duke University Libraries. Classic (2007).**
- Idea: an open-book mark whose longer right arm is meant to suggest growth, progress and change — [Duke University Libraries magazine, 2007](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/magazine/2007/10/new-logo)
- Why it matters: one small asymmetry is enough to make a generic open book ownable.

**R4. Forbes Library (Northampton, MA).**
- Idea: the logo takes the sandstone arch over the library's front door and makes it hint at open book pages — [Forbes Library](https://forbeslibrary.org/?p=25394)
- Why it matters: the book is merged with something specific to the institution (its architecture). For Servos, the equivalent would be something specific to oral and written translation.

**R5. Roxane Gay Books (imprint), by Pentagram. Current.**
- Idea: a rectangular "O" that suggests a book or page, and also a doorway or window — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/roxane-gay-books/story)
- Why it works: a letter of the name does double duty as the symbol, so it needs no extra pictogram. That's relevant because the Servos interface already uses heavy Mulish.

**R6. HarperCollins. Classic (1989/1990).**
- Idea: flames above waves. It merges Harper & Row's torch and Collins's fountain after the merger — [Wikipedia: HarperCollins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HarperCollins)
- Date conflict: most sources say 1989; one says 1990 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HarperCollins); [marefa.org](https://www.marefa.org/HarperCollins)
- Readings such as "fire = knowledge, water = movement" are commentators' interpretations, not company statements — [Design Observer](https://designobserver.com/undercover-branding/)
- Why it matters: it's the classic example of two opposite elements stacked into one compact, symmetrical sign (see Q3).

**R7. Storytel (audiobooks and e-books), identity by B-Reel. 2019.**
- Idea: the identity is built from letters, framed as the basic building blocks of stories — [B-Reel case study](https://www.b-reel.com/storytel)
- Audio: a separate audio brand built from voices in Storytel's audiobook library (Efterklang/AFRY) won Gold at Transform Awards Europe 2021 — [AFRY newsroom](https://www.afconsult.com/en/newsroom/news/efterklang-awarded-transform-awards-europe-2021)
- Why it matters: Storytel doesn't draw a book or a sound wave at all. The "spoken" side lives in the sonic identity, not in the symbol.

### Inferences
- All the strong book marks (R1, R2, R5) add one specific twist to the book: a monogram inside, a motion, or a letter-as-page. None is a literal open book with text lines. That's exactly where the owner's rejected "book with thin grey text lines" fell short.
- R1 and R5 suggest that letters (Mulish heavy or Charis SIL) can carry the "written" half without drawing lines of text.
- R2 shows that the curves of turning pages are visually close to wave curves, a possible bridge between "book" and "sound" without adding a separate audio icon.

### Gaps
- I couldn't open a Brand New (UnderConsideration) review of an open-book publisher or library mark directly. Search returned only general archive pages ([UnderConsideration FPO archive](https://underconsideration.com/fpo/archives/project-type/books/index.php?page=4)). Darien's Brand New coverage is known only second-hand through Tame the Web.
- I didn't verify reading-app icons (Apple Books, Google Play Books, Libby, Kindle) against a source, so they're excluded.

## 2. Which marks depict voice/audio/sound in a reduced way, and which avoid the generic "equaliser" or "volume" icon?

### Takeaway
The strongest audio marks either cut sound down to almost nothing (ElevenLabs: two vertical bars that are also "11"
and a pause sign) or fuse it with a second, ownable idea (SoundCloud: a cloud made of waves; Deezer: an equaliser
shaped as a heart; Sonos: a moiré that pulses on scroll). Bars or arcs on their own read as a generic equaliser or
wifi. Deezer explicitly dropped its earlier equaliser logo.

### Cited Findings

**R8. ElevenLabs (AI voice). Current.**
- Idea: the brand's shorthand is an "11" icon built from two simple vertical lines. It abstracts the number in the name and also subtly references the pause icon seen when listening to audio — [ElevenLabs brand guidelines](https://elevenlabs.io/brand)
- Rules: minimum clearance equal to the logo's height; the icon sits centred in a container three times its height; the wordmark must not be stacked as two words — [ElevenLabs brand page (Korean edition)](https://elevenlabs.io/ko/brand)
- Why it works: about the most reduced audio sign possible. It works at 16 px and in one colour, and it carries a double meaning (name + pause). It's ownable because the meaning comes from the name, not from a generic audio pictogram.

**R9. Spotify. Icon 2013, current.**
- Idea: three curved lines in a circle, read as sound waves and connectivity. The 2013 redesign set three white curved lines in a green circle — [Fabrik Brands](https://fabrikbrands.com/spotify-logo-history-spotify-symbol-meaning/)
- Colour: primary green is usually given as #1DB954, though one source lists #1ED760. The icon is used in green, black or white — [Fabrik Brands](https://fabrikbrands.com/spotify-logo-history-spotify-symbol-meaning/); [DesignRush](https://www.designrush.com/best-designs/logo/spotify-logo)
- Why it works: the arcs are horizontal, tapered and enclosed in a disc, so they don't read as a wifi fan. Lesson for Servos: arcs need a container or a fixed orientation, or they become wifi (the owner's "bridge" problem).

**R10. SoundCloud. Cloud since launch; orange since 2010.**
- Idea: a cloud whose left side is drawn as lines; a refinement made those lines look more like sound waves. The 2010 update brought in "SoundCloud orange" — [Fabrik Brands](https://fabrikbrands.com/branding-matters/logofile/soundcloud-logo-history/)
- The official logo didn't change in 2024; instead users can pick alternative app icons — [SoundCloud community](https://community.soundcloud.com/playbook-articles/choose-your-own-soundcloud-app-logo-with-new-designs)
- Why it works: the audio sign (stepped lines) is fused into a second shape that matches the name (cloud). It's a silhouette made of bars, which is one way to show "a book made of sound".

**R11. Sonos, by Bruce Mau Design. Identity 2011; "pulsing" campaign around 2015.**
- Idea: a static wordmark whose sunburst of lines makes a moiré that seems to pulse like a speaker when the page scrolls — [The Drum](https://www.thedrum.com/news/2015/01/23/sonos-unleashes-pulsing-optical-illusion-logo); [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/the-new-sonos-logo-looks-like-pulsing-sound-as-you-scro-1681318145)
- Intent is disputed: one report says the effect isn't mentioned in the agency's own description; another quotes BMD saying the sharp lines stood for high-fidelity sound and the pulsing was noticed afterwards — [Creative Bloq](https://creativebloq.com/branding/new-sonos-identity-11514065)
- Awards: a Cannes Lion and Adweek's "Cleverest Logo of the Year" — [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/news/sonos-logo-scrolling)
- Why it matters: sound is suggested by behaviour (vibration), not by a waveform icon. It doesn't apply directly to a 16 px icon, but it's an argument for putting "audio" into motion (splash or recording state) rather than into the static mark.

**R12. Deezer, by Koto. November 2023.**
- Idea: a heart built from rhythmic shapes, described as a mix of a pounding heart and a musical equaliser. It replaced the earlier, pure-equaliser logo — [Creative Review](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/deezer-koto-rebrand-graphic-design/); [Design Compass](https://designcompass.org/en/2023/11/10/deezer-rebrand)
- Motion: the logo moves between two states, one tied to a heartbeat and one to music — [Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/06-november-10-november-2023/koto-deezer-identity/)
- System: a custom typeface, Deezer Sans, takes its forms from the logo's shapes; a signature "Deezer purple" — [Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/06-november-10-november-2023/koto-deezer-identity/); [Deezer newsroom](https://newsroom-deezer.com/2023/11/deezer-new-brand-identity-and-logo/)
- Koto's award entry claims +47% brand searches in France and +36% free-to-paid conversions. These figures are self-reported and unverified — [Cannes Lions entry](https://lovethework.com/work-awards/campaigns/deezer-rebrand-1630423)
- Why it matters: the clearest documented case of a brand leaving the generic equaliser by bending the bars into a second, emotional shape.

**R13. Dwell (audio Bible app). Current.**
- (observed) A cluster of thin, slanted parallel bars of different lengths, like an italic waveform, whose outline forms a rounded, heart-like silhouette. It sits beside an italic serif "Dwell" wordmark in near-black on a warm off-white page — [dwellapp.io](https://dwellapp.io/)
- The site calls Dwell an audio Bible app — [Dwell site title](https://dwellapp.io/)
- Why it matters: a sector example that, like Deezer, turns bars into a shape. The hairline bars risk filling in at small sizes (my observation, not tested).

### Inferences
- Patterns that avoid the generic equaliser: (a) the bars mean something else as well (ElevenLabs "11"/pause); (b) the bars build a second silhouette (SoundCloud's cloud, Deezer's heart, Dwell's heart-like shape); (c) audio is carried by motion or sound rather than drawing (Sonos, and Audible/Storytel sonic logos in R14 and R7).
- Free-standing concentric arcs are the riskiest audio sign for Servos. They read as wifi unless they're enclosed or anchored (Spotify) or emerge from a specific object (Audible, R14). This matches the owner's "bridge looked like wifi" result.
- Thin, evenly spaced bars at low contrast (like the rejected "thin ochre audio bars") are the generic equaliser case. The professional versions use few, solid elements (ElevenLabs uses two).

### Gaps
- I didn't research Otter.ai, Speechify, Amazon SayHi, Apple Podcasts, Pocket Casts or BBC Sounds against sources, so they're excluded.
- I found no LogoLounge report that names a sound-wave trend. The 2024 report only notes that brands lean less on the logo alone and add colours, wordmarks and sound — [The Dieline on LogoLounge 2024](https://thedieline.com/logolounge-releases-its-2024-logo-trends-report/)

## 3. Which marks fuse two ideas (book + voice, text + sound, two languages, translation) in one simple symbol?

### Takeaway
The best fusions use one silhouette that reads two ways. BibleProject's solid open book has a speech-bubble tail,
and Audible's chevron opens like a book under sound arcs. Weaker fusions place two icons side by side (Talking Bibles:
an outline book plus wifi-like arcs). BibleProject is the closest existing analogue to a "book that speaks", so Servos
has to look clearly different from it.

### Cited Findings

**R14. Audible (Amazon). Symbol long-standing; animated version by Fold7.**
- Idea: according to the agency that did Audible's sonic branding, the newly animated logo (by Fold7) is an orange chevron that opens like a book and gives off a glow, read as a lightbulb switching on — [Goldstein London case study](https://creativepool.com/goldstein/projects/sound-branding-audible)
- Sonic logo: a three-note melody matching the three syllables of "Au-di-ble" — [Goldstein](https://creativepool.com/goldstein/projects/sound-branding-audible)
- The "sound waves" reading of the arcs comes from a third-party logo site, not from Audible — [WhatTheLogo](https://whatthelogo.com/logo/audible/271172)
- (observed) The orange symbol sits to the right of a black lowercase "audible" wordmark on [audible.com](https://www.audible.com/). I couldn't zoom in to check the exact geometry.
- Why it works: the book (chevron/spread) and the sound (arcs) are one orange glyph in one colour. Audio is reinforced by the sonic logo rather than by more drawing.

**R15. BibleProject. Current.** Strongest sector analogue.
- (observed) A solid cyan silhouette. Its top edge dips into a shallow V like the spread of an open book, and its bottom edge has a small triangular tail at the lower left like a speech bubble. One shape, no lines, no inner detail — [BibleProject brand resources](https://bibleproject.com/brand/)
- Colour and usage: the primary logo is the blue icon with black letters, on white; use a white version on dark backgrounds; don't squash or change it; brand blue is HEX #00B3E5 — [BibleProject brand resources](https://bibleproject.com/brand/)
- The icon is meant for short references and works better than the full logo at small sizes — [BibleProject brand resources](https://bibleproject.com/brand/)
- Why it works: book + speech in a single filled shape, one colour, readable at favicon size. Caution for Servos: this is the "open Bible that speaks" idea already executed by a well-known Bible-engagement brand. A Servos mark built as "book silhouette + speech tail" would look derivative.

**R16. Lutheran Bible Translators. Current.**
- (observed) A teal outline ring with a tail (a speech bubble) that encloses a solid open book. A two-weight stacked wordmark sits below, "Lutheran" light and "Bible Translators" bold — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/)
- Why it matters: book + speech by enclosure. It works because the book is solid and the bubble is a heavy ring. Risk: the bubble-first read is close to the "messaging app" problem the owner already hit with two speech bubbles.

**R17. Talking Bibles International. Current.**
- (observed) A grey line-drawn open book with concentric arcs above it, next to a "TALKING BIBLES" wordmark — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/)
- Why it's a cautionary example: it's the literal "book + sound" combination in thin outlines, and the arcs read as a wifi symbol. It's close to Servos's rejected concepts and shows why that route looks amateur.

**R18. Davar Partners International (audio Scripture). Current.**
- (observed) Wordmark "DAVAR", then a short vertical waveform of bars, then a large gold "D" whose bowl the waveform seems to sit in. Small tagline about God's word for all to hear — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/)
- Why it matters: it fuses the letter with audio. But the thin bars plus small tagline won't hold at small sizes (my reading).

**R19. Spoken Worldwide. Current.**
- (observed) The "o" of the lowercase "spoken" wordmark is an orange circle with sound arcs coming out of it, like a speaker; the rest of the letters are dark teal — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/)
- Why it matters: "text that sounds" placed inside a word. It's a letter-substitution approach that suits an app whose interface type is Mulish heavy, but the arc detail is small.

**R20. Google Translate. Icon 2010; "G" version 2015.**
- Idea: two overlapping squares (cards), one with a Latin letter and one with a Chinese character "文". In September 2015 the "A" became "G" to match Google's new typeface — [Logos-World](https://logos-world.net/google-translate-logo/); [Android Headlines, 2015](https://www.androidheadlines.com/2015/09/google-begins-changing-logos-for-services-icons.html)
- Why it works: translation as two overlapping tiles carrying two scripts. Simple, flat and understood worldwide. It depends on glyphs, though, and Servos's languages (e.g. Kichwa) use Latin script on both sides.

**R21. Wikipedia "puzzle globe". Classic (first version from a 2000 Nupedia competition; later refined).**
- Idea: an incomplete globe of jigsaw pieces, each carrying a glyph from a different writing system to symbolise multilingualism — [Wikipedia: Wikipedia logo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia_logo)
- Some glyphs were rendered wrongly (Devanagari, katakana, one Chinese character) — [Wikipedia: Wikipedia logo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia_logo)
- Why it's instructive: rich in meaning but far too detailed for an app icon. It also shows the risk of putting real scripts in a mark that native readers will check.

**Fusions listed under other questions:**
- R6 HarperCollins: two predecessor symbols (torch and fountain, i.e. fire and water) stacked into one compact sign — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HarperCollins)
- R12 Deezer: two meanings (heart and equaliser) in one shape, with motion switching between the two — [Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/06-november-10-november-2023/koto-deezer-identity/)
- R5 Roxane Gay Books and R22 Bible Society (England & Wales): one shape read several ways (book, page, door, window, and for Bible Society also a cross) — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/roxane-gay-books/story); [Bible Society](https://biblesociety.org.uk/latest/news/why-does-bible-society-have-a-different-look)

### Inferences
- There are two working fusion strategies:
  1. **One silhouette, two readings**: BibleProject (book/bubble), Audible (book/arcs as one glyph), Deezer (heart/equaliser), Bible Society (cross/book/door/window), Roxane Gay Books (O/page/door).
  2. **Letter as carrier**: DK (monogram inside book), Spoken ("o" as speaker), Davar ("D" + waveform), ElevenLabs ("11" as pause).
- "Icon A next to icon B" (Talking Bibles) is the amateur version. So is "book with lines and bars inside" (the rejected Servos concept).
- Since BibleProject already owns "book + speech tail", Servos's room is more likely in: the page curve doubling as a sound wave (Darien-type page motion), the book's spread or spine doubling as a waveform or "pause" (ElevenLabs-type double meaning), or a letterform. This is an inference, not a sourced finding.
- I found no professional mark that fuses two languages without using real glyphs (Google Translate, Wikipedia both rely on scripts).

### Gaps
- Translation companies: I found no reliable design sources for DeepL, Phrase, Lokalise, Smartling, Unbabel or memoQ. Phrase's 2022 rebrand (Memsource + Phrase) is documented only as a name and product change, without logo rationale — [Phrase news](https://phrase.com/news/memsource-and-phrase-announce-a-new-joint-identity/). One low-reliability page claims DeepL's logo has been stable since 2017 — [Design Your Way](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/deepl-logo/)
- I found no logo-design source for CLEAR Global (formerly Translators without Borders), only its 2021 formation — [ReliefWeb](https://reliefweb.int/organization/clear-global)
- I couldn't check Audible's exact geometry visually (the zoom failed); I rely on the agency description.

## 4. Bible translation and Bible engagement: how are the symbols built, which are well designed, which are clichéd, and what is overused?

### Takeaway
Most of the sector draws open books, globes, crosses, rays and plants, often in thin lines or several colours, and
many agencies use a wordmark only. The few that stand out use one solid, single-colour shape with a double reading
(BibleProject, Bible Society E&W's cross-book-door-window) or a simple geometric form (Seed Company's ring/seed).
Servos should avoid the literal open book with rays, the globe and the stand-alone cross. The open book is already
very crowded among translation agencies.

### Cited Findings

**My tally from the FOBAI members page (about 40 member logos, observed 2026-10-17)** — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/)
- Open book in about 8 marks: American Bible Society, United Bible Societies, Pioneer Bible Translators, Digital Bible Society, The Word For The World, Talking Bibles, Lutheran Bible Translators, and probably Whole Word Institute.
- Globe or map in about 5: Ethnos360, DOOR International, JCBT, Voice of the Martyrs, Bibles for China.
- Plants (tree, wheat, leaves, seed) in about 5: Taylor University CSE, Bible League, Seed Company, Whole Word Institute, Jerusalem Seminary.
- Sound or voice in 4: Spoken Worldwide, MegaVoice, Davar, Talking Bibles.
- Rays or sunburst in 2 (ABS, The Word For The World); cross as the main motif in at least Crossway; fish (ichthys) in Community Bible Study.
- Wordmark only or nearly: Faith Comes By Hearing, Wycliffe Bible Translators (serif version), SIM, Lifewords, Jesus Film Project.
- These counts are my own reading of the page, not a published statistic.

**R22. Bible Society (England & Wales).**
- Idea: a cross that can also be read as a book (the Bible), a door to pass through, or a window to look through — [Bible Society](https://biblesociety.org.uk/latest/news/why-does-bible-society-have-a-different-look)
- Date: one search summary put the refresh in 2023, but I couldn't confirm it. The page returned 404 when fetched on 2026-10-17, though search engines still index its text — [Bible Society](https://biblesociety.org.uk/latest/news/why-does-bible-society-have-a-different-look)
- Why it works: it takes the sector's most clichéd sign (the cross) and gives it several readings, so the mark is about invitation, not only identity.

**R23. American Bible Society.** (observed) A dark blue open book with a fan of rays behind it, a thin red line, and a stacked wordmark — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). Classic sector formula: book plus radiance. Recognisable, but the rays turn into texture at small sizes.

**R24. United Bible Societies.** (observed) An open book built from a mosaic of small multicolour squares over a wave line — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). The idea (many languages and peoples) is good, but there are too many colours and pieces for 16 px.

**R25. Wycliffe Global Alliance.** (observed) Three thick calligraphic arcs (orange, green, orange) nested like a "C" or turning pages, above a green wordmark — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/).
- National Wycliffe organisations use different logos. Wycliffe Australia's 2013 logo combined its earlier colours (red, blue, golden orange) with a book emblem; the organisation had used six logos in six decades — [Wycliffe Australia corporate report 2013](https://dev.wycliffe.org.au/documents/corporate-report-2013.pdf/)
- Why it matters: Wycliffe's global mark avoids the literal book by using arcs, but its brand is fragmented across countries.

**R26. SIL Global.** (observed) Two adjacent squares: a light-blue one with a single white handwritten stroke, and a dark-blue one with "SIL" in white. The site's tagline is "Life in Every Language" — [SIL Global site](https://global.sil.org/); [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/)
- SIL was founded in 1934 and works through language research, translation, training and materials — [Benevity charity listing](https://causes.benevity.org/causes/840-751840827?lang=es-es)
- Why it matters: "written" is shown by a pen stroke (handwriting), not by a book. It's simple, but the two-square lockup is a block, not a free-standing symbol.

**R27. Seed Company.** (observed) A green ring that breaks and curves inward into a seed or leaf form, with a light grey "Seed Company" wordmark — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). Launched by Wycliffe in 1993 to accelerate Bible translation — [OSCAR](https://oscar.org.uk/organisations/seed-company)
- Why it works: one colour, one geometric gesture, scales well. It shows a sector brand can skip the book entirely.

**R28. Pioneer Bible Translators.** (observed) A solid orange square with white text and a small line-drawn open book under "Pioneer" — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). It works as an app-icon block, but the line book is generic.

**R29. Digital Bible Society.** (observed) An open book whose pages fan upward like sails, in several shades of blue — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). Distinctive but multi-tone and detailed.

**R30. The Word For The World.** (observed) A gold open book with radiating lines, above a serif wordmark — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). The textbook cliché: book plus rays.

**R31. Scripture Union International.** (observed) A teal square with a single white line drawing of a flame or leaf shape above a cupped curve — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). It reduces well to a container icon, but the single thin line is fragile at small sizes.

**R32. Biblica (The International Bible Society).** (observed) A teal disc with "Biblica" in white and a small cross above the "i" — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). The name Biblica dates from 2009 (merger of IBS and Send the Light) — [MNN](https://www.mnnonline.org/news/international-bible-society-and-send-the-light-ibs-stl-become-biblica/)

**R33. Faith Comes By Hearing / Bible.is.** (observed) A wordmark only, "Faith Comes By Hearing" in maroon bold sans — [FOBAI members page](https://forum-intl.org/membership/members/). The ministry began as Hosanna in 1972 and became Faith Comes By Hearing in 1989 — [FCBH Our Story](https://faithcomesbyhearing.com/about/our-story). Bible.is launched in 2008 with audio and text — [faith.tools](https://faith.tools/app/bible-is-faith-comes-by-hearing)

**R34. YouVersion (Bible App).**

### Inferences

## 5. What patterns recur in the professional marks?

### Takeaway
The professional marks share six traits: one colour, solid shapes rather than hairlines, a single idea with a second
reading, few elements, rules for small sizes and clearance, and a system around the symbol (a typeface, motion and
sound) that carries what the static mark leaves out.

### Cited Findings
- **Single colour that adapts:** DK's logo became single-colour so it can take any colour or finish — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story). Spotify's icon is used in green, black or white — [DesignRush](https://www.designrush.com/best-designs/logo/spotify-logo). BibleProject specifies a white version for dark backgrounds — [BibleProject](https://bibleproject.com/brand/)
- **An icon built for small sizes:** BibleProject's icon is for short references and works better small than the full logo — [BibleProject](https://bibleproject.com/brand/). ElevenLabs reduces its icon to two lines and specifies clearance equal to its height and a container three times its height — [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/brand); [ElevenLabs KO](https://elevenlabs.io/ko/brand)
- **Double reading / one gesture:** pause + "11" (ElevenLabs); heart + equaliser (Deezer); cross + book + door + window (Bible Society); page-flip + wave/leaf/wing (Darien) — [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/brand); [Creative Review](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/deezer-koto-rebrand-graphic-design/); [Bible Society](https://biblesociety.org.uk/latest/news/why-does-bible-society-have-a-different-look); [Geissbuhler](https://geissbuhler.com/darien-library)
- **Evolve, don't discard equity:** DK is a simplification of its previous open-book mark — [Pentagram](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story). SoundCloud kept its cloud for over a decade and added optional app icons instead of a redesign — [SoundCloud community](https://community.soundcloud.com/playbook-articles/choose-your-own-soundcloud-app-logo-with-new-designs)
- **The system carries the rest:** Deezer's custom typeface takes its forms from the logo — [Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/06-november-10-november-2023/koto-deezer-identity/). Audible pairs its symbol with a three-note sonic logo — [Goldstein](https://creativepool.com/goldstein/projects/sound-branding-audible). Storytel's audio brand uses library voices — [AFRY](https://www.afconsult.com/en/newsroom/news/efterklang-awarded-transform-awards-europe-2021). LogoLounge's 2024 report notes brands relying less on the logo alone and more on colour, wordmarks and sound — [The Dieline](https://thedieline.com/logolounge-releases-its-2024-logo-trends-report/)
- **Motion as meaning:** Deezer's logo moves between heartbeat and music states — [Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/issues/06-november-10-november-2023/koto-deezer-identity/). Sonos's static lines pulse optically on scroll — [The Drum](https://www.thedrum.com/news/2015/01/23/sonos-unleashes-pulsing-optical-illusion-logo). Audible's chevron is animated opening like a book — [Goldstein](https://creativepool.com/goldstein/projects/sound-branding-audible)
- **Letters as symbol:** DK's monogram inside the book, Roxane Gay Books' rectangular O, Storytel's letter-built identity — [Pentagram DK](https://pentagram.com/work/dk/story); [Pentagram RGB](https://pentagram.com/work/roxane-gay-books/story); [B-Reel](https://www.b-reel.com/storytel)

### Inferences
- How this maps to the owner's rejected concepts:
  - "Book with thin grey text lines and thin ochre bars in a blue rounded square": it breaks four patterns at once (hairlines, three colours, two separate icons inside a container, literal depiction).
  - "Words joined by alignment lines" (read as "XI") and "two speech bubbles" (read as messaging): both are outcomes of a second reading that wasn't controlled. The professional double readings (R8, R12, R22) are chosen on purpose and stay in the same field (audio, music, faith).
  - "Bridge" (read as wifi or a table): free arcs carry generic meanings; see R9 and R17.
- A professional route for Servos would likely be: one solid, single-colour silhouette of an open book whose spread or page edge also reads as a sound wave or voice gesture. It shouldn't be a speech-bubble tail (owned by BibleProject, R15) or loose arcs (wifi, R17). Test it at 16 px and in one colour, and let a Mulish-heavy wordmark, a short animation (page turn to wave) and possibly a short sonic cue carry the rest. This is a synthesis, not a sourced fact.
- Mulish heavy (interface) and Charis SIL (scripture) could carry the written/spoken duality typographically (Charis = written word, Mulish = voice/interface), as DK and Roxane Gay Books let letterforms carry meaning. This is a design inference.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures "works at 16 px" across these marks; the small-size judgements are mine, based on observation.
- I found no source that ranks translation-industry logos (DeepL, Phrase, Lokalise, Smartling, Unbabel, memoQ), so they couldn't serve as pattern evidence.
- Studio case studies from Koto, Collins, DesignStudio, Wolff Olins, Mucho, Lundgren+Lindqvist and Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv specifically about books, reading or translation weren't found within the search budget, apart from Koto/Deezer.
