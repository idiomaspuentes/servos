# How professionals build a vector logo mark and adapt it to app icons and favicons (for the Servos open-book mark)

Scope note: research done 2026-10-17. Official platform guidance (Apple HIG, Android, Google Play, Material, web.dev) and studio/practitioner sources were consulted. Older sources (before 2023) are flagged inline. Items marked "Inference" are the researcher's own application to Servos and are not claims made by a source.

## 1. Process: from concept to final vector mark, and what separates amateur from professional

### Takeaway
Identity studios treat the mark as problem solving. They define goals first, sketch many alternatives by hand, reduce hard, and only then construct digitally and refine. The professional result is one simple, distinctive silhouette that carries meaning over time. Amateur marks show their pieces: several small parts, illustration instead of a sign, clichés, and the "logo dropped into a rounded square" look.

### Cited Findings
- Chermayeff & Geismar treat logo design as problem solving. The first task is to define the issues and the goals; marketing analysis is only one piece and reflects what has been, not what could be. (Tom Geismar interview, Nov 2009, older.) — [Logo Design Love: Tom Geismar interview](https://www.logodesignlove.com/tom-geismar-interview)
- Geismar sketches first, by hand. Sketching is faster for trying variations and easier for curves; the computer jumps too early to a sharply defined object. The studio studies "a great many alternatives" and never presents designs it cannot stand behind. — [Logo Design Love: Tom Geismar interview](https://www.logodesignlove.com/tom-geismar-interview)
- Sagi Haviv (partner, Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv) also says a hand-held pencil or pen is usually the best way to turn an idea into a visual. The firm's book *Identify* shows sketches and rejected variants for dozens of marks. — [Logo Design Love: C&G&H 60 years](https://www.logodesignlove.com/chermayeff-geismar-haviv-60-years)
- Haviv's commonly cited criteria are that a mark be appropriate, distinctive and memorable, and simple. A mark should pass a "doodle test" (people can draw it from memory) and work at every pixel size. The hard part is walking the line between simplicity and distinctiveness. (Secondary summaries of his talks and interview; wording varies by source.) — [Amara: The 3 Rules of Good Logo Design](https://production-blue.amara.org/videos/Jd0UC4Jk5HxE/info/the-3-rules-of-good-logo-design); [Logo Design Love: Sagi Haviv interview](https://www.logodesignlove.com/sagi-haviv-interview)
- Paul Rand's 1991 essay argues that what a logo means matters more than what it looks like, and that a logo identifies rather than sells directly. (Older; summarised from the essay page and secondary quotes. The full text could not be fetched in this session.) — [paulrand.design: Logos, Flags, and Escutcheons](https://paulrand.design/writing/articles/1991-logos-flags-and-escutcheons.html); [Jim Nielsen: Paul Rand on logos](https://blog.jim-nielsen.com/2016/paul-rand-on-logos/)
- Michael Bierut (Pentagram) argues that much of what people see in a logo happens in the viewer's mind. Meaning is built over time through association, not put into the drawing on day one. — [It's Nice That: Michael Bierut on logos (Design Indaba)](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/michael-bierut-logos-indaba); [Rosetta: Michael Bierut (Vox interview description)](https://rosetta.to/person/michael-bierut)
- The FedEx mark (Lindon Leader, 1994) went through hundreds of iterations before the arrow in the negative space was found. Sources disagree on the count: about 200 versions narrowed to six, or more than 400. Leader said the arrow was not an initial idea. It was discovered during refinement and then built in deliberately with custom letterforms. — [Retail Brew, 2022](https://www.retailbrew.com/stories/2022/10/07/logo-big-or-go-home-how-fedex-s-secret-arrow-was-created-accidentally); [CNN: hidden designs in famous logos](https://edition.cnn.com/style/article/hidden-designs-famous-logos)
- Reduction as a method (Michael Flarup, app icon designer): strip out details until the concept starts to fade, then check whether recognition improved. Line variations up side by side on a grid. Look at what competitors do and go in a different direction. (Smashing Magazine, Jan 2017, older.) — [Smashing Magazine: Designing better app icons](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2017/01/designing-better-app-icons/)
- Flarup says a company logo placed inside a square is never a good app icon. Logos and icons are different things: icons are outputs built for a square canvas at specific sizes and contexts. Look for a mark that works within those constraints instead of forcing the logo in. — [Smashing Magazine (2017)](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2017/01/designing-better-app-icons/)
- Flarup's four aspects of app icon design are scalability, recognizability, consistency and uniqueness. — [Skillshare: Designing App Icons (Flarup)](https://www.skillshare.com/en/classes/designing-app-icons/2137389708)
- Construction grids are often a presentation device, not the origin of the form. The claim that Apple's logo was built from golden-ratio circles is a myth. Rob Janoff drew from real apple cross-sections, and you can fit circles onto almost any shape after the fact. Designers sometimes overlay circles afterwards to impress clients. — [Fast Company: Debunking the myth of Apple's golden ratio](https://www.fastcompany.com/1672682/debunking-the-myth-of-apple-s-golden-ratio); [Cult of Mac](https://www.cultofmac.com/227961/was-apples-logo-really-designed-using-the-golden-ratio/); [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/news/most-overused-shape-graphic-design)
- Generic clichés cannot be rescued by small tweaks such as a new colour combination. Commonly listed clichés include lightbulbs, globes, swooshes, ticks, initials inside shapes and abstract human figures. Generic marks are also harder to protect as trademarks. — [Inkbot Design: logo design clichés](https://inkbotdesign.com/logo-design-cliches/); [LogoGround](https://logoground.com/get-logos-approved.php); [DesignCrowd: generic and overused concepts](https://www.designcrowd.com/help/article/generic-common-and-overused-logo-concepts-and-how-to-avoid-them)

### Inferences
- The studio process, mapped to Servos:
  1. Write a one-line brief: "an open book whose two pages are writing and voice."
  2. Fill pages of thumbnail sketches, in black only, at about 2 cm.
  3. Pick 3–5 directions and redraw each as a 16 px silhouette before any detail.
  4. Construct the survivors on a grid in vector.
  5. Refine with optical corrections.
  6. Test the mark at 16, 32, 48 and 180 px, on light and dark, in a tab bar and on a home screen, beside competitor icons.
  7. Only then add colour.
- The earlier Servos attempts match the amateur symptoms the sources name:
  - Many separate small parts. The sources call for one focal element and a single silhouette.
  - Grey or semi-transparent strokes. Apple asks for opaque source layers, and favicon guidance asks for extreme contrast.
  - A logo inside a rounded square. Flarup says outright that this is never a good icon.
  - Wavy sine strokes. They read as illustration, not as a sign.
- The fix is reduction and consolidation, not added polish.

### Gaps
- No current (2023–2026) step-by-step process write-up from Pentagram, Koto, Collins, Wolff Olins, DesignStudio or Instrument was retrieved. Their case studies are mostly visual and were not fetched. The process above rests on C&G&H (2009), Flarup (2017) and Leader accounts.
- Haviv's exact wording of his criteria comes from secondary sources. The book *Identify* itself was not consulted.

## 2. Construction rules: grids, keylines, stroke and counter widths, radii, Béziers, optical corrections, negative space

### Takeaway
Professional marks are built from few geometric primitives on a grid. They use one stroke weight (or a deliberate, systematic contrast), a single corner-radius system, and minimal, well-placed Bézier nodes. They are then corrected by eye: overshoot for rounds, optical centring, and thinning or traps at tight joints. Grids and keylines support the work; they don't make a mark good on their own.

### Cited Findings
**Grids and keylines (icon systems you can borrow from)**
- Material system icons use a 24 × 24 dp grid. Content sits in a 20 × 20 dp live area with 2 dp of padding. Extra visual weight may spill into the padding, but nothing goes outside the trim area. Keyline shapes (circle, square, rectangles, orthogonals, diagonals) give consistent proportions. (M2 page, via search snippet; the page did not render when fetched.) — [Material Design: System icons](https://m2.material.io/design/iconography/system-icons.html)
- Material system icons use a consistent 2 dp stroke for curves, angles and interior and exterior strokes, with a default 2 dp corner radius. (Secondary sources only.) — [Ulster University blog on Material icon principles](https://blogs.ulster.ac.uk/bethbuchanan/?p=2768); [Singapore Government Design System: iconography](https://designsystem.tech.gov.sg/foundations/iconography)
- Material product icons are drawn at 400% (192 × 192 dp) so that edges fall on 4 dp increments. At 100% that becomes 48 dp, which keeps edges sharp and aligned. — [Material Design: Iconography principles](https://m2.material.io/design/iconography)
- GitHub Primer Octicons (via search snippet of the official guidelines; the page did not render when fetched):
  - Two hand-drawn versions per icon, at 16 px and 24 px.
  - A fixed 1.5 px stroke at both sizes.
  - Round caps and joins, with a 1 px corner radius. Small filled parts such as arrowheads get 0.25 px.
  - A 1 px gap where objects overlap, and 1.5 px around modifiers.
  - Outer edges snapped to the pixel grid; inner edges left unsnapped.
  - Reference shapes (circle, square, rectangle) to match "optical volume" across icons.
  - [Primer: Octicons design guidelines](https://primer.style/octicons/guidelines/design)
- Microsoft Fluent 2: product launch icons have a 48 px threshold. Below 48 px they simplify detail; above it they use full fidelity. They scale in steps of four (48 → 192). They are meant to be used at specific sizes rather than scaled. — [Fluent 2: Iconography](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/iconography)
- Material Symbols has an optical-size axis (20–48 dp) that changes stroke thickness by size so icons look consistent. Its weight axis runs from 100 to 700, and a separate grade axis (−50 to 200) fine-tunes weight without changing size much. — [Google Fonts: Material Symbols guide](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/material_symbols)

**Bézier hygiene (from type design, which applies directly to logo outlines)**
- Put nodes at the horizontal and vertical extremes, where the tangent is horizontal or vertical. This often lets you delete intermediate points. — [Glyphs: Drawing good paths](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)
- Each curve segment's handles must stay inside the segment's "magic triangle". Handles must not cross each other or the other handle's extension. Avoid one-handle ("zero handle") curves. — [Glyphs](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)
- Avoid unnecessary inflection points. Make consecutive curves share curvature at the node (G2 continuity) for smooth joins. — [Glyphs](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)
- Typical handle lengths fall between just under 60% and above 70% of the segment's span. A circle sits near 55–56%. — [Glyphs](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)
- Remove superfluous nodes, zero-length segments and "double overlaps". Outer contours run counter-clockwise and counters run clockwise. Close all paths. — [Glyphs](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)
- For opposing curves (the inner and outer edge of a curved stroke), give the inner curve more curvature than the outer one. Otherwise the stroke looks like a flat tyre. — [Glyphs](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)

**Optical corrections**
- A circle drawn the same nominal size as a square looks smaller because it has less area. In one worked example, a 126 × 126 px circle matches a 120 × 120 px square (about +5%). Adjust in roughly 1 px nudges. (Marvel blog, c. 2016, older.) — [Marvel: Optical adjustment – logic vs. designers](https://marvelapp.com/blog/optical-adjustment-logic-vs-designers/)
- Type designers use overshoot: round letters extend slightly past the x-height and baseline so they don't look small. — [Marvel](https://marvelapp.com/blog/optical-adjustment-logic-vs-designers/); [MyFonts: optical illusions in geometric font design](https://cms.myfonts.com/content/optical-illusions-and-eye-trickery-in-geometric-font-design)
- There is no single correct overshoot percentage. A type designer's test found the best-looking value depends on viewing size and is partly subjective. — [TypeDrawers: Too much overshoot?](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/comment/27492)
- A geometrically centred triangle (a play button) looks off-centre because its mass sits on its flat side. Nudge it by eye. — [Pixso: icon alignment](https://pixso.net/articles/alignment-icon/); [Marvel](https://marvelapp.com/blog/optical-adjustment-logic-vs-designers/)
- In icon families, non-square shapes (triangles, arrows, linear icons) are enlarged and squarish ones get more padding so their visual sizes match. — [UX Collective Bootcamp: 5 things to learn from type designers](https://bootcamp.uxdesign.cc/5-things-to-learn-from-type-designers-while-dealing-with-visuals-5e4f4762159d)
- Ink traps are notches cut into tight inside corners. Without them, spreading ink or blur fills the joint into a dark blob. Carter's Bell Centennial is the classic case. Traps become more necessary as size decreases. — [CBA Design: Ink traps](https://cba-design.com/italy/en/insights/ink-traps/); [UX Collective: Ink traps and pals](https://uxdesign.cc/ink-traps-and-pals-56452af2c395); [High-Logic: create ink traps](https://www.high-logic.com/font-editor/fontcreator/tutorials/create-ink-traps)
- Apple asks for minimal shapes, filled and overlapping shapes, and no extremely thin line weights or sharp corners. WWDC25 adds that sharp edges and thin lines should ideally be avoided. Use bolder line weights for elements that get the glass treatment, and rounder corners so light travels along edges. — [Apple HIG: App icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons); [WWDC25 session 220](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/220/)

**Negative space**
- The FedEx arrow is formed in the negative space between letters. It was refined until it read as a genuine arrow, yet fewer than one in five people reportedly notice it unaided. A clever hidden form is therefore a bonus, not the main carrier of recognition. — [Retail Brew](https://www.retailbrew.com/stories/2022/10/07/logo-big-or-go-home-how-fedex-s-secret-arrow-was-created-accidentally)

### Inferences (concrete rules for drawing the Servos mark in SVG)
**Grid**
- Draw on a 48-unit (or 96-unit) square viewBox, e.g. `viewBox="0 0 48 48"`. That way 1 unit equals 1/3 px at 16 px, 1 px at 48 px, and 10.67 px at 512 px. Snap outer edges to whole units.

**Stroke weight**
- Use one stroke weight for every line in the mark. If the mark must survive at 16 px with at least about 1.5–2 px strokes (the Primer and Material figures), the stroke must be at least 1/8–1/10 of the mark's width. On a 48-unit grid with the mark about 40 units wide, that means a stroke of about 4–5 units.
- Gaps and counters must be at least the stroke width. Otherwise they close up at small sizes, which is the ink-trap logic.
- Count across the width: at 16 px only about four stroke-plus-gap pairs fit. So no more than about 2–3 text lines on one page and 2–3 audio bars on the other.

**Shapes and fills**
- Prefer filled shapes (solid pages) with lines cut out as negative space, rather than outline strokes. Apple explicitly favours filled, overlapping shapes and warns against thin lines. A filled page with cut-out lines survives as one silhouette at 16 px.

**Corners and caps**
- Pick one corner-radius rule, for example outer page corners radius equal to the stroke, and inner corners equal to 0 or stroke/2. Use one cap style throughout: round caps for both the text lines and the audio bars, or square caps for both.

**Curves**
- Draw the page curves (the top edges of an open book) with nodes only at the extremes and at the spine. Use 2–4 nodes per page edge, symmetric handles, and no inflection points.

**Optical corrections**
- Where the two pages meet at the spine (a tight V-shaped joint), thin the joint or open a small notch, an ink-trap analogue, so the spine doesn't become a dark blob at 16–32 px.
- Make any round element (such as a circular voice dot) about 3–5% larger than its square counterpart.
- If the mark is asymmetric (text page versus audio page), centre it optically, not by its bounding box.

### Gaps
- The exact keyline dimensions of Material product icons and of IBM's icon grid could not be retrieved: the pages rendered empty. The Material 2 dp stroke figure is confirmed only by secondary sources.
- No source in this session documented the general type-design rule that horizontal strokes must be thinner than vertical strokes to look equal, or gave a numeric value for it. It is standard practice in type design but unsourced here.
- No source gave a numeric rule for how much to thin strokes where they meet in an icon or logo. Ink-trap sources are typographic.

## 3. Depicting an open book plus an audio waveform without clip-art clichés; fusing two ideas without getting busy

### Takeaway
Good fusions use one shape that reads two ways: books on a shelf that are also equalizer bars, a chevron that opens like a book, or a letter "g" whose bowl is an open book. Two separate pictures side by side don't count. The "Bible plus cross plus sound waves" combination is a well-worn religious-media cliché.

### Cited Findings
- The Talking Books logo (Adrian Walsh, Canadian audiobook retailer, 2011, older) is one mark that reads as a row of books on a shelf and as the equalizer bars of a stereo. The reviewer contrasted it with the busy original, which used a globe and extra initials. — [BP&O: Talking Books logo](https://bpando.org/?p=2993)
- Audible's animated logo (by Fold7) is an orange chevron that opens like a book and emits a glow, which together suggest a lightbulb switching on. Reading it as "sound waves" comes from weaker aggregator sources. — [Goldstein London / Creativepool: Audible sound branding](https://creativepool.com/goldstein/projects/sound-branding-audible); [logotyp.us: Audible](https://logotyp.us/logo/audible/)
- In July 2025 Goodreads introduced its first new logo since 2007. It is a chunkier lowercase "g" that also serves as the app icon. It combines a magnifying glass over an open book, and was described as built for accessibility so it stays clear from phone screens to billboards. — [Goodreads Blog](https://www.goodreads.com/blog/show/2984); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91368403/goodreads-logo)
- AudioVerse (a Christian audio-sermon ministry, logo from 2008) combines a Bible outline, a cross and three sound waves coming from the cross, which deliberately echo the RSS/podcast symbol. This is an example of the literal "Bible + waves" combination. — [AudioVerse: Our new logo](https://audioverse.org/en/blog/68/our-new-logo)
- Apple recommends focusing on one element with a minimal number of shapes, and suggests designs based on filled, overlapping shapes. In the 2025 redesigns Apple reduced overlap ("less is more") so the material's intersections and edges could show. — [Apple HIG: App icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons); [WWDC25 session 220](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/220/)
- At 16 px a mark is a single silhouette. If it needs two separate elements to make sense, it has no workable 16 px version. (Practitioner newsletter, Sep 2026.) — [Iconic by IconBuddy #8: Logos are just icons with a job](https://iconic.iconbuddy.com/p/iconic-08-logos-are-just-icons-with-a-job)
- Flarup: centre the icon on one object instead of cramming in several ideas. The world doesn't need another generic symbol such as a checkmark. — [Smashing Magazine (2017)](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2017/01/designing-better-app-icons/)
- Logos with descriptive elements (visual cues to the product) were linked to better evaluations for unfamiliar brands, because they are easier to process and feel authentic. This supports keeping the book recognisable rather than going fully abstract. — [Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood 2019, JMR (repository)](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705); [AIGA Eye on Design, 2019](https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/researchers-want-to-help-you-design-more-successful-logos-but-do-they-have-good-advice/)

### Inferences (ownable fusion ideas for "one page written, the other spoken")
- **Same primitive, two orientations.** Draw both pages as one filled open-book silhouette. Cut horizontal lines (text) out of the left page and vertical bars (sound) out of the right page, all with the same width, the same caps and the same spacing. The visual rhyme says "the same word, written versus spoken". It stays one silhouette at 16 px, where the cut-outs simply disappear and the book shape remains. This takes the Talking Books shelf-equals-equalizer idea one step further.
- **The page as the waveform.** Let the top edge of the right page be stepped or rise and fall like a waveform envelope, made of straight segments or bars, while the left page has a calm, flat top edge. The book silhouette carries the audio idea with no extra pieces.
- **Bars, not sine waves.** Sine-wave strokes of uniform width with round caps read as organic forms (the "worms" complaint). Vertical bars of varying height are the established sign for recorded or digital audio (equalizer, waveform). Keep the bar count small (3–5) and the heights in a clear rhythm, for example a symmetric rise and fall, not random.
- **Avoid:** crosses, doves, globes, rays of light, speech bubbles, headphones, microphones and Wi-Fi/RSS arcs added to a book. Each adds a separate piece, and the "Bible + sound waves" combination is already in use (AudioVerse). For a descriptive yet ownable mark, the book itself must carry both meanings.
- **Test:** cover the left half, then the right half. Each half should still read as half a book, and the whole should read as one object.

### Gaps
- No systematic study or credible guide on "metaphor fusion" in logo design was found. The evidence is case-based (Talking Books, Audible, Goodreads, FedEx).
- The current icons of comparable Bible and audio-scripture apps (YouVersion, Bible.is, Faith Comes By Hearing's Render, SIL Scripture App Builder) were not reviewed for differentiation. This would help avoid look-alikes.

## 4. Scalability: 16 px favicons to 512 px icons, micro versions, minimum stroke and gap, monochrome first, reversed versions

### Takeaway
Professionals design the small version as a first-class drawing, not a scaled-down copy. Below about 48 px they simplify: fewer elements, thicker strokes, wider gaps. They check the mark in one colour and both light-on-dark and dark-on-light. They make reversed (light-on-dark) versions slightly lighter, because light shapes on dark grounds look heavier and glare.

### Cited Findings
- Fluent 2 sets 48 px as the threshold below which product icons simplify detail, and asks for drawings at specific sizes rather than scaling. — [Fluent 2: Iconography](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/iconography)
- Primer draws every icon twice (16 px and 24 px) with a fixed 1.5 px stroke and 1–1.5 px minimum gaps. It snaps outer edges to whole pixels. — [Primer: Octicons design guidelines](https://primer.style/octicons/guidelines/design)
- Material Symbols changes stroke thickness by optical size (20–48 dp). For a light symbol on a dark background it recommends a lower grade (example −25) to reduce glare. — [Google Fonts: Material Symbols guide](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/material_symbols)
- Responsive logo practice (Viget, 2015, older):
  - Smooth intricate shapes, thicken thin strokes, and turn outlined elements into filled or inverted ones.
  - Remove small type step by step.
  - Move the mark beside the wordmark.
  - At the smallest size, drop the wordmark and enlarge the mark.
  - [Viget: Responsive logos part 1](https://viget.com/articles/responsive-logos-part-1-tips-for-adapting-logos-for-small-screens)
- Responsive logo systems usually have 3–4 variants that differ in size and level of detail, down to the symbol alone at the smallest size. (Agency blog.) — [Clay: How to prepare your logo for responsive design systems](https://clay.global/blog/responsive-logo-design)
- Favicon practice:
  - Design the favicon first and scale up.
  - Use one shape and keep text out.
  - Use extreme contrast (two values); subtle gradients turn to mush in a tab.
  - Test on both light and dark tab bars.
  - Put your tab next to Gmail, GitHub and Figma and check whether it can be found by icon alone.
  - [Iconic by IconBuddy #8 (Sep 2026)](https://iconic.iconbuddy.com/p/iconic-08-logos-are-just-icons-with-a-job); [10Web: favicon design](https://10web.io/blog/favicon-design/)
- A logo "stress test" checks that the mark stays identifiable at favicon size (16 × 16) and in 100% black and white, with no greys. — [Inkbot Design: logo testing](https://inkbotdesign.com/logo-testing)
- Evil Martians recommends one 32 × 32 favicon.ico and an SVG icon, and adding a 16 × 16 layer only if the 32 px version blurs when scaled down. If 16 × 16 is too blurry, ask the designer for a version tailored to the small pixel grid. — [Evil Martians: How to favicon (updated 2024–2026)](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/how-to-favicon-in-2021-six-files-that-fit-most-needs)
- Half-pixel offsets cause blur. A 1 px line centred on a pixel boundary renders as two 50% grey pixels. Integer stroke widths on matching coordinates stay crisp, and fractional widths such as 1.5 at small sizes are the worst case. (WHATWG list, 2013, older but still accurate.) — [WHATWG: Blurry lines in 2D Canvas (and SVG)](https://lists.whatwg.org/pipermail/whatwg-whatwg.org/2013-August/082720.html)
- Apple: dark, clear and tinted icons are more subdued, so keep them visible, legible and recognisable. Keep the same features across appearances; don't swap elements. Base the dark icon on the light one. — [Apple HIG: App icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)
- Monochrome as a basic requirement: commentators summarise Rand's philosophy as a logo that identifies reliably, is simple and scalable, and works in black and white. (Secondary.) — [Inkbot Design: Paul Rand](https://inkbotdesign.com/paul-rand/)

### Inferences
- Plan three drawings of the Servos mark:
  - **Full** (≥ 64 px): book plus text lines plus audio bars.
  - **Small** (24–48 px): the same, with at most two lines and three bars, thicker strokes and wider gaps.
  - **Micro** (16–20 px): book silhouette only, or the silhouette with a single slot per page, hand-snapped to a 16 px grid.
- Minimum sizes at 16 px: features ≥ 2 px, gaps ≥ 2 px, outer edges on whole pixels.
- Build the mark in one flat colour first, using only black, white and the brand ink. No grey and no opacity.
- Make a reversed version (light mark on ink or dark blue) with strokes about 5–10% thinner, or gaps slightly wider. This follows the Material grade logic; the percentage is a starting point, not a sourced number.

### Gaps
- No authoritative source gives a universal minimum stroke or gap in px for logos, as opposed to UI icon systems. The 1–2 px figures come from Primer and Material icon systems.
- The "do not use the main logo below 24 px" threshold appeared in a search summary of agency blogs but could not be tied to a specific verified page.

## 5. App icon specifics: Apple (Liquid Glass, dark/clear/tinted), Android adaptive, PWA maskable, Google Play, favicons

### Takeaway
Supply unmasked square artwork and let each platform apply its own mask, shadows and effects. Keep the main content well inside the safe zones: Android logo 48–66 dp of 108 dp; PWA maskable inside a centred circle whose diameter is 80% of the icon. For Apple, provide layered vector artwork: a background plus 1–4 foreground groups, flat and opaque, with appearance variants. Provide a monochrome layer for Android themed icons and Apple tinted icons, and a dark-aware SVG favicon.

### Cited Findings
**Apple (HIG, change log: 9 Jun 2025 layered icons/Liquid Glass; 8 Jun 2026 refined Liquid Glass guidance)**
- **Canvas sizes:**
  - iOS, iPadOS and macOS: 1024 × 1024 px square, masked by the system to a rounded rectangle.
  - watchOS: 1088 × 1088 px, masked to a circle.
  - visionOS: 1024 × 1024 px, circular.
  - tvOS: 800 × 480 px.
  - Colour spaces: sRGB, Gray Gamma 2.2 or Display P3.
  - [Apple HIG: App icons (JSON source)](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons.json); [Apple HIG page](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)
- **Layers:** a background layer plus one or more foreground layers. Icons on iOS, iPadOS, macOS and watchOS take on Liquid Glass: specular highlights, refraction and translucency. — [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)
- **Six appearances:** default, dark, clear light, clear dark, tinted light and tinted dark. The system generates any you don't provide. — [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons); [WWDC25 session 361](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/361/)
- **Layer artwork:**
  - Prefer clearly defined edges, with no feathering.
  - Import fully opaque layers and set opacity in Icon Composer.
  - Prefer vector (SVG or PDF) and convert text to outlines.
  - Use a solid or gradient background (full-bleed and opaque if imported).
  - Don't pre-mask; pre-masking makes edges jagged and hurts the specular effect.
  - Keep primary content centred.
  - Don't add your own highlights, shadows, bevels, blurs or glows; the system adds dynamic ones.
  - [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)
- **Design:** embrace simplicity, use a minimal number of shapes, and you don't need to fill the canvas. Include text only if essential. Avoid extremely thin line weights and sharp corners. — [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)
- **WWDC25 (session 220):**
  - New grid with a rounder corner radius, and a designated frame for circular artwork.
  - Favour flat, frontal perspectives.
  - Reduce overlap and baked-in effects; avoid sharp edges and thin lines; use bolder weights.
  - Use the System Light and System Dark gradients instead of pure white or black.
  - Lean towards coloured backgrounds in the default icon so switching to dark mode reads clearly.
  - [WWDC25: Say hello to the new look of app icons](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/220/)
- **Icon Composer (session 361):**
  - Export each layer at canvas size, as SVG for flat vectors or PNG for gradients and raster.
  - Never include the rounded-rectangle or circle mask.
  - Number layers in Z order.
  - Groups: one by default, up to four.
  - For simple icons: one foreground and one background, with colours separated into layers so dark mode only needs a fill change.
  - Mono/tinted: set at least one element to white and map the other colours to greys.
  - Keep source art flat, opaque and easy to control.
  - [WWDC25: Create icons with Icon Composer](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/361/)

**Android adaptive icons**
- All layers are 108 × 108 dp. The outer 18 dp on each side is reserved for masking and effects such as parallax and pulsing. The inner 66 × 66 dp is the safe zone that OEM masks never clip. — [Android Developers: Adaptive icons](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/launch/icon_design_adaptive)
- The logo should be at least 48 × 48 dp and must not exceed 66 × 66 dp. — [Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/launch/icon_design_adaptive)
- Provide foreground and background layers (vectors preferred), plus a single monochrome layer for themed icons (Android 13 / API 33+). From Android 16 QPR 2 the system auto-themes apps without one. Use clean edges with no masks or shadows around the outline. — [Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/launch/icon_design_adaptive)

**Google Play store icon**
- 512 × 512 px, 32-bit PNG, sRGB, at most 1024 KB. Upload a full square: Play applies a mask with a corner radius of 30% of the icon size, plus the drop shadow. No transparency in the background. Freeform logos sit on the keyline grid. No text or graphics indicating ranking, deals or Play programs. — [Google Play icon design specifications](https://developer.android.com/distribute/google-play/resources/icon-design-specifications)

**PWA maskable icons**
- Keep important content inside a centred circle with a radius of 40% of the icon width (80% diameter). The outer 10% may be cropped. — [web.dev: Maskable icons (last updated 2019-12-19, older but still the reference)](https://web.dev/articles/maskable-icon)
- Don't combine `"any maskable"`. Using a maskable icon as "any" adds needless padding. Design "any" icons like favicons, with transparency and no extra padding. — [web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/maskable-icon)
- Manifest set: icon-192.png, icon-512.png, and a separate 512 × 512 icon with `purpose: "maskable"` whose logo sits in a centred 409 × 409 safe zone. Check it at maskable.app. — [Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/how-to-favicon-in-2021-six-files-that-fit-most-needs)

**Favicons**
- Minimal set: favicon.ico (32 × 32, linked with `sizes="32x32"` to avoid a Chrome bug), icon.svg, apple-touch-icon.png (180 × 180), and the manifest. A dark mode for the SVG favicon is done with `@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark)` inside the SVG's `<style>`. — [Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/how-to-favicon-in-2021-six-files-that-fit-most-needs)

### Inferences
- Safe-zone arithmetic for a 512 px maskable icon:
  - The safe circle is 409.6 px in diameter.
  - A roughly square mark inscribed in it can be at most about 290 px (409.6 / √2).
  - Android's 48–66 dp of 108 dp is 44–61% of the canvas.
  - So aim for the open-book mark's bounding box at about 45–55% of the canvas width (≈ 230–280 px on 512), visually centred, on a full-bleed brand background.
- For the "any" icon and the favicon, the mark should fill the canvas edge to edge, with no tile and no padding beyond about 1 px. This avoids the "icon in a rounded square" look in browser tabs.
- For Apple and Liquid Glass:
  - Make the open book one foreground group (two if the written page and the spoken page should get different glass depth).
  - Use a solid or gradient ink background.
  - Use white or near-white for the book, so the mono/tinted mapping works.
  - No hairlines; every cut-out at least about 4% of 1024 (≈ 40 px). This last figure is the researcher's estimate from the 16 px rule, not an Apple number.
- Android monochrome layer: the same book silhouette with its cut-outs, in a single colour. Because it uses filled shapes, the themed icon stays legible.
- Tauri desktop: reuse the 1024 master to generate .ico/.icns. Hand-check the 16, 24, 32 and 48 px outputs.

### Gaps
- Apple's HIG text gives no numeric safe-zone or keyline values for the new iOS 26 grid; they live only in the Apple Design Resources templates, which were not inspected.
- The web.dev maskable article has not been updated since 2019. No newer W3C or Chrome guidance changing the 40% radius was found.

## 6. Symbol and wordmark: lockups, clear space, matching the typeface, optical sizing

### Takeaway
Brand guidelines tie lockup spacing and clear space to a unit taken from the logo itself, such as the x-height of a letter or the height of the symbol. They align the symbol to the wordmark optically, not by bounding box. They balance the visual weight of the symbol and the type.

### Cited Findings
- Scale AI's guidelines:
  - The unit "e" is the height of the lowercase e in the wordmark.
  - Clear space is at least 1e on all sides; more is better.
  - The lockup gap is 3e.
  - Every partner lockup needs an optical-balance check, since partner marks differ in weight and proportion.
  - All-caps marks align to cap height and baseline.
  - [Scale brand: Logo](https://brand.scale.com/logo)
- Asana: clear space equals the height of the symbol. The horizontal lockup is primary; the vertical lockup is for constrained spaces. — [Asana brand guidelines](https://asana.com/es/brand)
- Serco: clear space is derived from the "O" of the wordmark and scales with the lockup. — [Serco: Clear space](https://www.serco.com/brand-guidelines/clear-space2)
- Metafy aligns its symbol and wordmark optically rather than metrically, because the symbol has a heavy base and lighter points. — [Metafy brand: Logo](https://brand.metafy.gg/latest/brand/logo-gakad5JR)
- Afterpay's co-branding guidance keeps logos at the same visual weight, with similar letter size and weight for paired wordmarks. — [Afterpay brand tools: collaborations](https://afterpay.com/en-US/for-retailers/resources/marketing/brand-tools/brand-collaborations)
- FedEx's wordmark was custom-drawn by combining Univers 67 and Futura Bold with ligatures, so the negative-space arrow would work. This shows symbol and type being shaped together. — [Retail Brew](https://www.retailbrew.com/stories/2022/10/07/logo-big-or-go-home-how-fedex-s-secret-arrow-was-created-accidentally)

### Inferences
- Servos's wordmark is set in Mulish 800–900. Match the mark's stroke (or cut-out) width to the stem width of Mulish ExtraBold or Black at the lockup size. Measure a vertical stem of "r" or "v" at the chosen size and use that width for the book's lines and bars. Mirror Mulish's terminals: if the bars have round caps, check that they harmonise with Mulish's soft, near-flat terminals; square caps may match better.
- Size the symbol so its optical height sits between the cap height and about 1.3× the cap height of "Servos". The book is wider than it is tall and visually light at the top, so it usually needs to be slightly taller than the cap height to look equal. Align it optically, not by its bounding box.
- Define a lockup unit (for example the x-height of "e" in "Servos"). Then set the gap between symbol and wordmark (about 2–3 units) and the clear space (at least 1 unit, ideally the symbol height) in that unit.
- Provide a horizontal lockup (primary), a stacked lockup, and the symbol alone. The symbol alone is what goes in icons.

### Gaps
- No authoritative general source was found that prescribes matching symbol stroke weight to the wordmark's stem weight. The practice above is inferred from type-design logic and brand-guideline examples.

## 7. Vector hygiene for delivery: anchors, outlined vs live strokes, pixel snapping, formats

### Takeaway
Keep a working file with live strokes. Deliver outlined, filled paths with as few nodes as possible, nodes on extremes and integer or grid coordinates, in a clean SVG with a viewBox and no transforms. Export rasters per size, hand-checking the small ones.

### Cited Findings
- SVG supports only centred strokes. Inside or outside strokes must be exported as outlines or clipped. Offsetting a path to imitate a stroke "isn't viable". Use primitives (`rect`, `circle`, `ellipse`) where possible. Resolve transforms into coordinates. Include a viewBox. Make width and height optional. Use hex sRGB colours. Omit generator comments and layer-name IDs. Convert short text to paths. Around 3 decimals is enough for production assets. (Marc Edwards / Bjango, Apr 2022.) — [Bjango: Ideal SVG exports](https://bjango.com/articles/svgexports/)
- For logo artwork that others will scale, outline strokes in the final file: live strokes don't scale with the artwork in many tools. Keep live strokes only in the working file. (Adobe community answer; consensus rather than an official standard.) — [Adobe Community: Illustrator SVG discussion](https://community.adobe.com/t5/illustrator-discussions/some-paths-disappear-when-saving-as-svg/m-p/12165055)
- `vector-effect="non-scaling-stroke"` keeps stroke width constant under transforms. It is a display tool, ignored by Illustrator, and not suitable for logo masters. — [Vectorizer.ai output options](https://vectorizer.ai/api/outputOptions); [Call Me Nick: SVG vector effects](https://dev.callmenick.com/posts/svg-vector-effects)
- Apple asks for vector layers (SVG or PDF) with text converted to outlines, because SVG doesn't preserve fonts. Use PNG only for gradients and raster art, with no mask applied. — [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons); [WWDC25 session 361](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/361/)
- Snap outer edges to whole pixels; leave inner edges free (Primer). Fractional stroke widths and half-pixel offsets blur at small sizes. — [Primer](https://primer.style/octicons/guidelines/design); [WHATWG list (2013)](https://lists.whatwg.org/pipermail/whatwg-whatwg.org/2013-August/082720.html)
- Clean outlines: minimal nodes, nodes on extremes, no zero-length segments, consistent contour direction, closed paths, overlaps removed on export. — [Glyphs: Drawing good paths](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)
- Favicon pipeline: square SVG master; ICO exported at 32 × 32 with 32 bpp and 8-bit alpha; add a hand-drawn 16 × 16 only if needed. — [Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/how-to-favicon-in-2021-six-files-that-fit-most-needs)

### Inferences
- Deliverables for Servos:
  - `mark.svg`: single colour, outlined, one `<path>` per colour or a compound path with `fill-rule`, on a 48-unit viewBox with integer coordinates.
  - `mark-dark.svg`, or one SVG with a `prefers-color-scheme` style.
  - `mark-micro-16.svg`, pixel-fitted.
  - `icon-any-512.png`, `icon-maskable-512.png` and `icon-192.png`.
  - `apple-touch-icon-180.png` and `favicon.ico` (32, plus 16 if needed).
  - Android `ic_launcher_foreground`, `_background` and `_monochrome` vector drawables on 108 dp with the logo at 48–66 dp.
  - An Apple `.icon` from Icon Composer: SVG layers at 1024.
- Generate the files from a script in the repository; the project already does this for icons. Commit the master SVG as the single source of truth.
- Build cut-outs (text lines and bars) as holes in the page shape (compound path, `fill-rule="evenodd"`, or boolean subtract), not as separate shapes painted in the background colour. Holes stay correct on any background and in monochrome or tinted modes.

### Gaps
- Bjango's article on pixel fitting (bjango.com/articles/pixelfitting) returned 404, so no current icon-specific pixel-hinting guide was retrieved. The pixel-snapping evidence rests on Primer and a 2013 WHATWG thread.

## 8. Evidence on memorable and effective logos, and on app store icon tests

### Takeaway
Academic evidence favours marks that are recognisable as something real ("natural" or descriptive), harmonious (balanced, symmetric, coherent) and only moderately elaborate. This matters especially for unknown brands. Visual complexity that is attractive at first can wear out with repeated exposure. App store tests show icons can move installs either way, and changing away from a recognised mark can hurt. Published icon-only uplift numbers are scarce and mostly vendor marketing.

### Cited Findings
- Henderson & Cote (1998, Journal of Marketing, older) scored 195 logos on 13 design characteristics:
  - High-recognition logos should be very natural, very harmonious and moderately elaborate.
  - High-image ("professional look") logos should be moderately elaborate and natural.
  - Low-investment logos should be less natural but very harmonious.
  - [Henderson & Cote, Guidelines for selecting or modifying logos (PDF)](https://newion.uwinnipeg.ca/~ssingh5/Lm/logo-guide.pdf)
- Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood (2019, Journal of Marketing Research): across six studies and an analysis of 597 logos, more descriptive logos improved brand evaluations, purchase intentions and brand performance. They are easier to process and feel more authentic. The effect is weaker for familiar brands and reverses for categories with negative associations. About 60% of brands use non-descriptive logos. — [JMR manuscript repository](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705); [AIGA Eye on Design, Oct 2019](https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/researchers-want-to-help-you-design-more-successful-logos-but-do-they-have-good-advice/)
- Designers' critique of this research: data should inform but not dictate, and formulas risk flattening creativity (Bobby C. Martin Jr., OCD). — [AIGA Eye on Design](https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/researchers-want-to-help-you-design-more-successful-logos-but-do-they-have-good-advice/)
- Miceli, Scopelliti, Raimondo & Donato (2014, Psychology & Marketing, older): visual complexity's initially positive effect on logo attitude turns negative with repeated exposure. Conceptual complexity's initially negative effect turns positive with repeated exposure. — [City Research Online: Breaking through complexity](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/4267/)
- Google Play case (Spiegel Online): replacing the traditional black-and-red mark with a modern monochrome icon decreased installs. The team attributed this to departing from the recognised brand mark. In the same programme, a description change raised daily installs by more than 10%. — [Android Developers: Spiegel Online story](https://developer.android.com/stories/apps/spiegel-online)
- Google Play case (Splendid Apps): testing the logo and screenshots by country increased store-listing visitors by 20%, and the updated graphics improved conversion. — [Google Play Console: Splendid Apps case study](https://play.google.com/console/about/splendid-casestudy/)
- SplitMetrics case (Nanobit): months of the team's own icon variants failed to beat the control, then a vendor-suggested variant won. The 18% organic conversion uplift is for the whole campaign, not the icon alone. (Vendor case study.) — [SplitMetrics: Nanobit](https://splitmetrics.com/customers/nanobit-increases-conversion-with-splitmetrics/)
- Flarup frames the icon as the product's visual anchor, which should communicate the app's core essence. — [Adobe Blog: Michael Flarup interview (2015)](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2015/07/23/flarup-app-icon)

### Inferences
- For a new, unknown brand like Servos, the evidence supports a descriptive mark that people recognise as an open book over an abstract one. It should be harmonious (symmetric spine, rhythmic lines and bars, one stroke system) and only moderately elaborate. That rules out both the hyper-minimal abstract shape and the busy illustration.
- Since Servos is distributed offline (SD, USB or Bluetooth) rather than mainly through stores, store A/B testing matters less than recognisability on the home screen and in the browser tab. A cheap substitute for an A/B test: show the 3–5 finalists at 48 px on a real phone home screen to a few translators, then ask them a day later to draw or describe the icon. This is the doodle test.

### Gaps
- The original van Grinsven & Das (2016) paper on logo complexity and exposure could not be accessed. Secondary summaries conflict (one says complex logos gain recognition and appreciation over time; another reports no main effect), so it is not cited as a finding.
- No credible, current (2023–2026), icon-only conversion benchmarks from Storemaven or SplitMetrics were found. A claimed "20–30% swing from icon tests" appeared only on a third-party blog and could not be verified against Google.
